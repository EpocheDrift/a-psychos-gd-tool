# Brief and Art-Direction Translation

Use this reference when the person has a feeling but not a layout solution,
when two directions must be compared, or when an accepted system must become a
series.

## Contents

1. Progressive brief capture
2. Translating aesthetic language
3. Constructing distinct directions
4. Adapting a series
5. Presenting a direction

## 1. Capture the brief progressively

Build the following internal envelope from conversation:

```text
Job and real context:
Audience and viewing distance/time:
Feel first → understand next → act last:
Exact copy and assets:
Hard constraints:
More like / less like:
Accepted relationships to protect:
Current dissatisfaction:
Interpretive freedom:
Good-enough signal:
Unknowns that can materially change the result:
```

Do not expose this as a mandatory questionnaire. Ask one compact question only
when an unknown changes the likely direction, asset use, or production result.
Otherwise state a reversible assumption and proceed.

Treat these as hard constraints unless the person says otherwise:

- exact wording, spelling, punctuation, and required legal or event details;
- frame/aspect ratio and publishing or physical context;
- protected images, subjects, logos, and brand relationships;
- accessibility or legibility requirements;
- declared privacy and licensing boundaries.

Treat feeling words as hypotheses until translated into visible relationships.

## 2. Translate aesthetic language

Use the pattern:

```text
feeling → visible relationship → reason it serves the job
```

Examples:

| Human language | Candidate relationship | Avoid assuming |
| --- | --- | --- |
| quiet | fewer simultaneous focal points, controlled contrast, slower spacing | beige minimalism |
| loud | decisive scale contrast, compressed intervals, abrupt reading shift | random distortion |
| cool | specific cultural distance, restraint, or tension appropriate to context | one fashionable effect |
| absurd | formal seriousness applied to disproportionate content, or deliberate scale mismatch | illegibility |
| coordinated | shared axes and type roles with optical compensation | mechanical centering everywhere |
| less template-like | content-responsive topology and varied reading vectors | random movement |
| editorial | authored sequencing, type-image negotiation, deliberate pacing | magazine imitation |
| brutalist | exposed structure, blunt hierarchy, constrained rawness | default monospace plus black borders |

If the word has multiple plausible readings, define two opposing interpretations
before choosing form. Explain the opposition as a relationship, not a style
label.

Keep geometric and optical judgments separate. A string can be mathematically
centered while punctuation, letter shapes, or surrounding contrast makes its
visual mass feel shifted.

## 3. Construct distinct directions

Hold these fixed across directions:

- content and hard constraints;
- asset set and frame;
- agreed audience, tone boundary, and functional hierarchy;
- capability and evidence conditions when comparing results.

Vary the organizing principle. Score no aesthetic winner in advance.

Use these axes:

1. reading path;
2. symmetry versus directional bias;
3. density versus active space;
4. scale allocation;
5. order versus disruption;
6. flat versus spatial behavior;
7. content-derived formal rule;
8. emotional intensity.

Require at least two observable axis differences between two proposed
directions. Reject a comparison if the only differences are typeface, color,
texture, or a single effect.

For each direction, write:

```text
Name: neutral working label
Organizing rule: one sentence
Reading sequence: first → next → last
Relationship to the content/context:
Two or more differing axes:
Primary risk:
What must stay identical to the other direction:
```

Do not use labels such as “safe” and “experimental” when the labels could bias
the person’s choice. Neutral labels are especially important in an evaluation.

## 4. Adapt a series

Declare three lists before laying out all members:

- **Protected** — content, asset, subject, crop, or context that may not change.
- **Invariant** — semantic roles, hierarchy, type/color roles, or rhythm that
  make the set recognizable.
- **Adaptive** — position, alignment, scale, density, copy length, reading
  vector, or image–type relation that should respond to each member.

Consistency should come from shared grammar, not copied pixel coordinates.
Every adaptive move needs a visible reason in that member’s content or image.

Inspect all members at equal scale before choosing member-specific title or
layout topology. If the images/previews are not available, stop at the
Protected / Invariant / Adaptive plan and name the visual cues to inspect;
do not assign one member to a corner, axis, split, or reading vector in
advance. Preassigned variety can become a second template.

Use a representative prototype only if one member exposes the major structural
risks. Otherwise design the whole set without pretending one image represents
all others.

Review each member at the same thumbnail scale, then review a contact sheet.
If no native contact-sheet tool exists, compose one outside the design document
from exact source previews and retain each preview’s revision/hash mapping.

## 5. Present a direction

Lead with the intended experience, not software operations:

```text
I read the job as…
The first/next/last experience is…
The design rule is… because…
I preserved…
I deliberately changed…
The main risk is…
```

When the person chooses or rejects a direction, preserve their words verbatim.
The reason is more reusable than the label.
