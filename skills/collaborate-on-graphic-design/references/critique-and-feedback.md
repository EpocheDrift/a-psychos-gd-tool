# Critique and Feedback Translation

Use this reference after a preview, when reviewing an existing design, or when
the person gives non-technical aesthetic feedback.

## Contents

1. Two-pass critique
2. Feedback translation
3. Optical and geometric diagnosis
4. Anti-template review
5. Focused revision and stopping

## 1. Critique in two passes

### Pass A — objective and requirement checks

Check before offering taste judgments:

- exact copy, punctuation, asset, and frame;
- intended layer/version visibility;
- current render revision and evidence ticket;
- unintended clipping or missing content;
- required first, second, and third reading levels;
- protected relationships and tone boundaries.

Do not praise aesthetic quality while a hard requirement is visibly wrong.

### Pass B — aesthetic and intentional checks

Review:

- first visual pull and reading path;
- hierarchy at thumbnail scale;
- weight distribution and active space;
- optical rather than merely geometric balance;
- type roles, spacing rhythm, line lengths, and edge behavior;
- whether effects have a semantic job;
- whether the organizing rule is memorable and content-specific;
- whether a series shares grammar without copying a template.

State observations as diagnoses, not universal taste facts. Prefer “the period
pulls visible mass to the right of this axis” over “this is badly centered.”

## 2. Preserve and translate human feedback

Record the original statement before interpreting it. Use:

```text
Original feedback:
Feeling named by the person:
Relationship most likely responsible:
Accepted relationships to protect:
One highest-priority change:
Why that change serves the stated goal:
Parameters left to the Agent:
Would this change the concept/mode? yes/no
```

Translate common statements cautiously:

| Feedback | Investigate | Do not automatically do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| “not coordinated” | shared axes, paired visual widths, type roles, vertical beats, optical compensation | center everything |
| “too much like a notice/template” | authorship, hierarchy convention, repeated topology, predictable margins | destroy legibility |
| “not creative/cool enough” | return to concept axes and content-derived rules | add rotation, warp, or grain |
| “this one area is strange” | local weight, punctuation mass, collision, alignment, contrast | rebuild the whole design |
| “make each one less repetitive” | preserve series roles; adapt topology to each member | randomize positions |
| “both are good” | retain both accepted variants and stop | force a ranking |

When the person cannot explain why, propose one or two relationship hypotheses
and, if useful, change one variable at a time. Do not demand design vocabulary.

If one feedback message contains both “keep this, correct one local relation”
and “now make the overall concept more original,” create two work items. Treat
the first as Refinement/Craft correction against a preserved checkpoint and
the second as a new Exploration. Do not fuse them by making the local defect
the concept unless the person explicitly approves that bridge.

## 3. Separate optical from geometric diagnosis

Use measurement for painted bounds and overflow. Use visual judgment for:

- punctuation and asymmetric glyph mass;
- image contrast that changes perceived weight;
- negative-space shape;
- baseline and cap-height relationships;
- apparent centering of unequal forms;
- occlusion or competing focal points.

Apply optical compensation only after naming what imbalance it addresses.
Small numerical asymmetry can produce stronger visual balance; do not treat a
non-zero offset as an error by itself.

## 4. Detect safe-template collapse

Ask:

1. Does the main formal move arise from this content, audience, or context?
2. Can the organizing rule be described without naming a trendy style?
3. Would replacing the copy with an unrelated title leave the design equally
   plausible?
4. Do two directions differ structurally or only cosmetically?
5. In a series, do major changes respond to each member, or repeat coordinates?

Flag template risk when the third answer is yes or when the only rationale is
“it looks designed.” Do not reject a restrained design merely for being
simple; ask whether its restraint is intentional and specific.

Never default to any style token learned from prior examples, including a
particular background, accent color, oversized heading, monospace face,
diagonal collision, corner metadata, grain, or brutalist distortion.

## 5. Revise one priority and stop cleanly

Unless the direction is rejected:

1. freeze accepted content and relationships;
2. name the highest-salience problem;
3. change the smallest relationship set capable of solving it;
4. repeat hard checks and view both preview scales;
5. ask for judgment in the person’s own terms.

Start a new direction or Session when the feedback changes the concept rather
than correcting it. Preserve the previous accepted state first.

Stop when the person reaches a stage-appropriate acceptance threshold. A
design can be “good for now” without being declared universally optimal.
Further revision requires a new observable goal, not the Agent’s discomfort
with stopping.
